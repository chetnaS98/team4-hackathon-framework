## 1. Why this exists

Two weeks into a project, a stakeholder asks: **"Why did you design it this way?"**

The failure we are designing against is this: *you leave the call with a solution already forming in your head, and no evidence in your notes to back it up.* It happens because we commit before we understand — we nod along, hear a feature request, and start sketching.

The cure isn't a longer call. It's knowing exactly **what to listen for**, so you gather the main pieces of information *before* you commit — and knowing **what layer of research** you're holding when you turn that into a design direction.

The answer you want to be able to give:

> "Because here's what we heard, here's what we know, and here's how it traces."

---

## 2. TRACES: what it is

TRACES is a memory device for the **six pieces of information you need from any requirement call before you commit to anything**.

| Letter | Name | The question | You have it when you can say… |
|---|---|---|---|
| **T** | **Trigger** | What happened, and when? | Why this is coming up *now* |
| **R** | **Result** | What changes, measurably? | What moves, for the user *and* the business |
| **A** | **Audience** | Who does this work, where, on what? | Who does it, who's affected, who decides |
| **C** | **Confidence** | What do we know vs. assume? | Which claims are fact, assumption, opinion |
| **E** | **Edges** | What limits the answer? | Constraints, edge cases, what's been tried |
| **S** | **Say-so** | Who decides, and by when? | Sign-off, review cadence, timeline |

**The principle:** a good call leaves traces. If your notes are missing one of these, that's your clue that you don't have the full case yet.

All six fit comfortably in the first ten minutes of a call.

> **An empty letter is a valid output.** It isn't failure — it tells you exactly what to ask in the follow-up.

## 3. The two-sided rule: ask every letter twice

This is the part people skip, and it's the most important mechanic in the framework.

**Every letter has two sides:**

- **The REQUEST** — the thing they are asking us to build.
- **The WORKFLOW** — the problem or process underneath it.

> The request tells you what they *think* the answer is.
> The workflow tells you what the problem *actually* is.
> **Ask only about the request, and you end up designing their solution.**

### Worked example: "We need a new tracking feature"

| | Ask about the REQUEST | Ask about the WORKFLOW |
|---|---|---|
| **T** | "What made a tracking feature a priority now? Who asked, and what prompted them?" | "What happens in the tracking workflow today? Walk me through the last time tracking went wrong." |
| **R** | "What does the business expect this to change? How would you know it worked?" | "If tracking worked well, what would be different for the person doing it? Less time, fewer errors, fewer follow-up calls?" |
| **A** | "Who asked for it? Who will use it, and who approves it?" | "Who does the tracking today, where, and on what device? Who depends on that information?" |
| **C** | "Is the need backed by data — tickets, complaints — or by someone's belief?" | "Is the workflow you've described something you've watched happen, or how you think it works?" |
| **E** | "What limits this? Budget, deadline, platform, what's in and out of scope?" | "Where does tracking break today? What data is missing or late? What's been tried to fix it?" |
| **S** | "Who decides on this feature, and by when? How often do we review?" | "Who owns the tracking process today? If it changes, who has to agree to work differently?" |

**Which side to weight, by scenario:**

- **Fresh start** → weight the **workflow**. You don't know the problem yet.
- **Informed by synthesis** → research usually covers the workflow already; weight the **request**. What's new about it, and does it fit what the research says?
- **Ad hoc** → get both where you can; at minimum, get the **trigger** for both.

## 4. The six letters in detail

### T · Trigger
**You have it when** you can say what happened, and when — for the request and for the workflow.

Trigger is the story of *why now*. Something happened: an escalation, a new deadline, a number that dropped, a complaint that reached someone senior.

- "What happened that made this a priority now?"
- "Walk me through the last time the problem occurred."
- "What's the urgency, and what happens if it slips?"

**Why it matters:** the trigger usually contains the real problem in compressed form, and it tells you which parts of the request are negotiable.

---

### R · Result
**You have it when** you can say what changes, measurably, for the user *and* the business.

Result is the number that moves. It has two sides, and they can disagree — which is itself worth surfacing.

- "What changes if this works? How would you know? What number moves?"
- "If this worked well, what would be different for the person doing the work?"

> **If they can't name a measurable result, you don't have it yet. Write it down as missing.**

**Why it matters:** the Result becomes your success criteria later — and the "so what" of any insight should connect to it.

---

### A · Audience
**You have it when** you can say who does this work, where, and on what; who's affected; and who decides.

Audience is wider than "the user". It covers the person doing the work, their environment and device, who's affected downstream, and who approves.

- "Who does this today, where, on what device?"
- "Who's affected downstream?"
- "Who approves? **Can we talk to them?**"

> **Point to watch:** the person asking for the feature is rarely the person doing the work.

---

### C · Confidence
**You have it when** you can say what we know and what we assume.

Confidence is a *habit*, not a question. For every claim you hear, ask yourself — or them — one thing:

> "Is this something you've **seen**, in data or feedback, or something you **believe**?"

Tag each claim as you go: **fact / assumption / opinion**.

- "Is the need backed by data — tickets, complaints — or by someone's belief?"
- "Is that workflow something you've watched happen, or how you think it works?"

**Why it matters:** this is the same tagging habit that becomes data / finding / insight in Part 2. It is the single most-skipped letter under time pressure.

---

### E · Edges
**You have it when** you can say what limits the answer: constraints and edge cases.

Edges are the technical, legal, data, integration and timing limits. The unusual cases. What must not change. And: **what has already been tried, and failed.**

- "What limits this? Budget, deadline, platform, scope?"
- "Where does it break today? What data is missing or late?"
- "What's been tried to fix it?"

**Why it matters:** "what's been tried" saves you from proposing the idea they already rejected.

---

### S · Say-so
**You have it when** you can say who decides what, how we stay in sync, and the project timeline.

- "Who signs off on what?"
- "What's the review rhythm?"
- "Are there fixed dates, and what's the reason behind them?"
- "Who owns this process today? If it changes, who has to agree to work differently?"

**Why it matters:** this is the other letter everyone forgets under time pressure, and the one that causes the most rework.

## 4b. The full question bank — what to ask, and what you must be able to answer

Section 4 gives you the letter. This is the working bank: every question worth asking on each side, and the sentence you should be able to write before design starts. If you can't write that sentence, the letter isn't done — put it in the open questions.

---

### T · Trigger

**Ask about the REQUEST**

- What made this a priority now? What prompted it?
- Who asked for it, and what happened just before they asked?
- Why this quarter and not last? What happens if it slips?
- Has this been asked for before? What happened that time?
- Is it tied to a specific event — a complaint, an escalation, an audit, a demo, a competitor, a renewal, a regulation?

**Ask about the WORKFLOW**

- What happens in this workflow today? Walk me through the last time it went wrong.
- How often does that happen? Is it getting worse, or has it always been like this?
- What does the person do when it breaks? What's the workaround?
- Who notices first, and what do they do about it?
- Has anything changed recently — the team, the volume, the tooling, the rules?

**You have T when you can write:**
> "This came up now because *[event]*, which happened *[when]*. In the workflow the problem shows up when *[situation]*, roughly *[frequency]*, and today people work around it by *[X]*."

**Red flags:** "it's always been a problem", with no event attached — the real trigger is usually political or budgetary, so keep digging. Or: the trigger is one senior person's recent bad experience.

---

### R · Result

**Ask about the REQUEST**

- What does the business expect this to change? How would you know it worked?
- What number moves, from what to what, over what period?
- What does "done" look like to you?
- Is anyone's target or incentive tied to this?
- If we shipped it and nothing changed, how would we find out?

**Ask about the WORKFLOW**

- If this worked well, what would be different for the person doing the work — less time, fewer errors, fewer follow-up calls, less anxiety?
- What are they spending time on today that they shouldn't be?
- What would make them say "this is better" rather than "this is different"?
- Do the business goal and the user goal point the same way here?

**You have R when you can write:**
> "For the user: *[outcome]*, visible as *[signal]*. For the business: *[metric]*, from *[baseline]* to *[target]*, by *[when]*. We'd know it failed if *[counter-signal]*."

**Red flags:** success stated as a deliverable ("a new dashboard") rather than a change. A business metric with no user-side counterpart, or the reverse — that gap becomes a design conflict later.

---

### A · Audience

**Ask about the REQUEST**

- Who asked for this? Whose problem is it, in their view?
- Who will use it, and who has to approve it?
- Who is this explicitly *not* for?
- All users, or a segment, region or tier?

**Ask about the WORKFLOW**

- Who does this work today? Role, seniority, training, tenure?
- Where do they do it — desk, floor, field, ward, on the move? On what device, what screen, what connection?
- What else is happening around them while they do it — interruptions, time pressure, someone waiting?
- Who depends on the output? Who's affected downstream if it changes?
- Is there more than one kind of person doing this, and do they do it differently?
- Who would have to work differently if we changed it — and would they agree to?
- **Can we talk to them?**

**You have A when you can write:**
> "*[Role]* does this, in *[environment]*, on *[device/context]*, about *[frequency]*. Downstream: *[roles]*. Decision maker: *[who]*. Access to users: *[yes / no / via whom]*."

**Red flags:** only ever hearing "the user", singular. No access to anyone who actually does the work. The requester and the user being treated as the same person.

---

### C · Confidence

**Ask about the REQUEST**

- Is the need backed by data — tickets, analytics, complaints, sales feedback — or by someone's belief?
- Who in the business believes this most, and who disagrees?
- What data exists that we haven't seen? Can we have it?
- If we're wrong about this, what does it cost?

**Ask about the WORKFLOW**

- Is the way you've described this something you've watched happen, or how you think it works?
- When did you last sit with someone doing it?
- Who would describe this workflow differently from you?
- Which part of this are you least sure about?

**You have C when you can write:**
> A claims log in which every statement carries a tag (fact / assumption / opinion), a source, and — for the riskiest three — a note on what breaks if it's wrong.

**Red flags:** everything stated with equal certainty. Nobody can name a source. A secondhand description of a workflow presented as observed fact.

---

### E · Edges

**Ask about the REQUEST**

- What limits this — budget, deadline, platform, team capacity?
- What's explicitly in scope, and out?
- What must not change — screens, flows, integrations, contracts?
- Any legal, regulatory, security, procurement, accessibility or data-residency constraints?
- Does it have to land with an existing release train or an external dependency?

**Ask about the WORKFLOW**

- Where does this break today? What data is missing, late, wrong, or keyed in by hand?
- What are the weird cases — the exceptions handled manually, "that one client", the month-end spike, the offline situation?
- What's been tried to fix it, and why didn't it stick?
- What happens upstream and downstream that we can't change?
- Which rules only exist in people's heads?

**You have E when you can write:**
> "Hard constraints: *[list]*. Must not change: *[list]*. Known edge cases: *[list]*. Already tried: *[what, when, why it failed]*."

**Red flags:** no edge cases named — there are always edge cases, so it means nobody has looked. "No constraints, build what you think is right" — there are constraints, you just haven't found them.

---

### S · Say-so

**Ask about the REQUEST**

- Who signs off on what? One person, or a committee?
- Who can say no after everyone else has said yes?
- What's the review rhythm — how often, with whom, in what format?
- Are there fixed dates, and what's the reason behind each? *(A date with a reason is a constraint. A date without one is a preference.)*
- What's the approval path — design review, legal, security, compliance, procurement?
- When do you need to hear back from us?

**Ask about the WORKFLOW**

- Who owns this process today?
- If the process changes, who has to agree to work differently — and who trains them?
- Who can change the rules inside the workflow — policy, thresholds, permissions?
- Is there a process owner who isn't on this call?

**You have S when you can write:**
> "Decision maker: *[who]*, for *[what]*. Influencers: *[who]*. Review cadence: *[rhythm]*. Fixed dates: *[date — reason]*. Process owner: *[who]*. Next checkpoint: *[when]*."

**Red flags:** "we'll figure that out later". A decision maker who has never been on a call. Dates with no reason attached.

## 5. Three extra clues for designers

TRACES works for any requirement call. As designers, add three asks that general frameworks miss:

| | Ask | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **+1** | **Constraints** | The technical, legal, data and timing limits that shape what we can design |
| **+2** | **Existing product / design system** | What we have to reuse, respect or stay consistent with |
| **+3** | **What's been tried** | And why it didn't work — saves you proposing the rejected idea |

## 6. Which call am I in? Two questions, three scenarios

Before any requirement call, ask one question:

> **"How much do I already know about *this specific problem*?"**

Knowing the client is not the same as knowing the problem. Your answer decides how you spend the call, and what you're allowed to commit to.

### The decision flow

```
Q1: Did I know this call was coming, with time to prepare?
      │
      ├── No ────────────────────────────────► #1 AD HOC  (stop here)
      │
      └── Yes ──► Q2: Does research or synthesis exist
                      on this exact problem, and can I read it first?
                          │
                          ├── Yes ──────────► #2 INFORMED BY SYNTHESIS
                          │
                          └── No, or only
                              from other modules ──► #3 FRESH START
```

> **Research from a different module doesn't count.** Don't assume past users, workflows or constraints carry over to a new problem.

### The three at a glance

| | **Fresh start** | **Informed by synthesis** | **Ad hoc** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nickname** | "New territory" | "Head start" | "Cold call" |
| **Gut feel** | "I know the client, but not this problem." | "I have some insights and a partial picture." | "No warning, no prep." |
| **Purpose** | **Discovering** — build the picture from scratch | **Refining** — sharpen, correct, fill in the picture | **Listening** — capture the basics and buy time |
| **What you have** | Client context, no research on this module/feature | Research + a known call topic | Nothing |
| **Weight the…** | Workflow | Request | Trigger, both sides |
| **Can commit to** | Process only | Process + a directional intent | A recap and a next step. Nothing else. |
| **Output** | Draft requirement one-pager | One-pager traced to insights | A recap — then it becomes Fresh or Informed |

### The rule that spans all three

> **The less context you have, the more the call is for listening, and the less it is for committing.**

Every activity below is tagged **[Solo]**, **[Team]** or **[Stakeholder]** — prep is not all solo work.

## 7. Scenario 1 — Fresh start ("New territory")

*Existing client, new module or vertical, no research on the requested work. Some time for light secondary research.*

**Mindset:** "We know the client, but not this problem." Don't assume past modules' users, workflows or constraints carry over.

### Before

| Activity | Who | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| Mine our history with the client | **[Solo]** | Past decisions, personas, research, design system, how they give feedback, what they pushed back on |
| Secondary research, 1–2 hrs | **[Solo]** | How the workflow typically runs, key roles, a glossary, regulations, analogous products, public docs, support articles, app reviews |
| Build an assumption list | **[Solo]** | "What I think I know" vs "what I need to find out" — this becomes your call guide |
| Request context from the PM / account lead | **[Team]** | SOW, budget, timeline pressure, politics, who really decides. Plus: what data exists — analytics, support tickets, sales feedback, anyone who worked on adjacent modules |
| *If there's time:* a short pre-read | **[Stakeholder]** | Agenda, who we'd like on the call (a decision maker **+** someone who does the work), and a request for artifacts: screens, docs, current process, tickets |

### During

**Open with:**
> "Our goal today is to understand the problem well enough to ask the right questions, not to jump to solutions."

**Focus:** ask every letter about the request *and* the workflow. Here, **the workflow matters most** — you don't know the problem yet.

| | Prompt |
|---|---|
| **T** | What made this a priority now? Walk me through the last time the problem occurred. |
| **R** | What changes if this works, for the user and the business? How would you know? What number moves? |
| **A** | Who does this today, where, on what device? Who's affected downstream? Who approves? Can we talk to them? |
| **C** | "Is this something you've seen, in data or feedback, or something you believe?" Tag fact / assumption / opinion. |
| **E** | Technical, legal, data, integration and timing limits. Unusual cases. What's been tried and failed? What must not change? |
| **S** | Who signs off on what? Review cadence? Fixed dates, and the reason behind them? |

**Technique:** ask for **stories and screen-shares** — "show me how you do it today" — rather than opinions. Cast wide, then narrow in on whatever energises or confuses you.

| ✅ Commit to | ❌ Avoid |
|---|---|
| A playback ("Here's what I'm hearing…") | Timelines |
| A recap within 24 hours | Scope |
| Open questions + a proposed approach | Effort |
| The next touchpoint | Specific solutions, or "yes, that's easy" |

**If pushed:** *"I want to get this right. Let us validate before we commit."*

### After

| When | Activity | Who |
|---|---|---|
| Same day | Clean up notes. Note which TRACES letters you have, which are partial, and which are still unknown. | **[Solo]** |
| Same day | Debrief with the PM and researcher. Flag what surprised you, and them. | **[Team]** |
| Within 24 hrs | Send a recap: "our understanding" + open questions. Ask them to correct it. | **[Stakeholder]** |
| Within 2 days | Draft the one-pager and the assumption log. | **[Solo]** |
| Ongoing | Save everything to the shared project context. | **[Solo]** |

> **The output of a fresh-start call is a draft requirement one-pager. Not a design.**

## 8. Scenario 2 — Informed by synthesis ("Head start")

*Existing client, research exists, you know the high-level topic of the call.*

**Mindset:** "We've done the homework, so don't waste their time re-asking." Use the call to **confirm, correct and extend** — and to catch solutions that contradict what we already know.

### Before

| Activity | Who | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| Pull every relevant research insight | **[Solo]** | Existing insights, workflows, personas, JTBDs |
| **Pre-fill TRACES with sources and confidence** | **[Solo]** | For each letter: what we know, where it came from, how confident we are |
| Align on scope and constraints | **[Team]** | The PM or lead confirms what's agreed, so you don't contradict existing commitments |
| *If there's time:* 1–2 hrs secondary research on the known scope | **[Solo]** | |
| *If there's time:* an assumption list as your call guide | **[Solo]** | |
| *If there's time:* a half-page "what we understand today" pre-read | **[Stakeholder]** | Ask them to correct it before the call |

### During

**Open with (keep it to 2–3 minutes):**
> "Before we dive in, here's what we understand from the earlier research. Please correct anything that's off or outdated."

**Focus:** research usually covers the **workflow** already. Spend most of your questions on the **request** — what's new about it, and does it fit what the research says? Fill in the brackets from your pre-filled TRACES.

| | Confirm with TRACES |
|---|---|
| **T** | "We heard [trigger] drove this. Still true?" What changed that prompted this request now — new data, an escalation, a deadline? |
| **R** | "Research points to [result]. Is that the target?" If the business goal differs from the user need, surface it. |
| **A** | "We studied [users]. Anyone we missed?" Any new roles or decision makers? |
| **C** | "Here's what's confirmed vs assumed. Agree?" Go insight by insight: **confirmed, challenged or new**. What evidence backs the client's own claims? |
| **E** | "Known constraints are [X]. Anything new?" Anything we assumed was flexible that isn't? |
| **S** | Who owns the decision, the review rhythm, the timeline. You're better placed to ask these now. |

**If they solutioneer** — i.e. the request contradicts an insight — **don't say no.** Say:
> "Interesting. In the research we saw X. How does this approach account for that?"

| ✅ Commit to | ❌ Avoid |
|---|---|
| Everything from Fresh start, **plus** | Final solutions |
| **Directional intent**: "This aligns with what we learned about [insight], so we'd tailor the direction around that" | Delivery dates before you've scoped the effort |
| A **next-step timeline**: "We'll share a direction by [date]" — after checking with the team | Re-asking what the research already answered |

### After

| When | Activity | Who |
|---|---|---|
| Same day | Update the insights base: tag each insight **confirmed / challenged / new / outdated**. | **[Solo]** |
| Same day | Flag contradictions to the researcher, who decides whether light research is needed. | **[Team]** |
| Within 24 hrs | Send the recap, with the one-pager showing **which insight supports each requirement**. | **[Stakeholder]** |
| Within 2 days | Decide: are the gaps small enough to design on with stated assumptions, or do they need research first? | **[Team]** |

> **The output here is a one-pager traced to insights. Every requirement points to the evidence behind it.**

## 9. Scenario 3 — Ad hoc ("Cold call")

*No prep, no agenda, maybe no domain knowledge. You're going in blind.*

**Mindset:** "My job is to listen well, capture accurately, and not get cornered."
**Biggest risks:** committing to something you don't understand, or leaving without the basics.

### Before — the 60-second kit

You can't prepare the call, but you can prepare **yourself**.

| Activity | Who |
|---|---|
| Keep the cheatsheet handy: TRACES questions in priority order | **[Solo]** |
| Any warning at all? Check the invite or thread for who's attending and what's been discussed | **[Solo]** |
| Skim the client's project folder for related context | **[Solo]** |

### During

**Open — contract the call:**
> "I want to make sure I capture this properly. Can I ask a few questions to make sure I understand?"

**Stance — "teach me":**
> "Assume I'm new to this. Walk me through it."

Ask for the glossary as terms come up.

**Focus:** ask about the request *and* the workflow where you can. At minimum, get the **trigger** for both.

#### Priority order — you may only get 10 minutes

| | Ask |
|---|---|
| **1 · T** | What's happening that made you call today? What's the urgency, and what happens if it slips? |
| **2 · R** | What does a good outcome look like for you? What does "done" look like? |
| **3 · S** | Who's asking, and who's deciding? Who else needs to weigh in? When do you need to hear back? |
| **4 · A** | Who's affected, who's the end user, and where do they work? |
| **5 · E** | Anything that can't change? Anything already tried? |
| **6 · C** | Is this from data, or an observation? *(Capture it, but don't push hard.)* |

**Why this order:** urgency and outcome first, who decides third, confidence last. Don't push hard in a call you weren't prepared for.

**Techniques:** paraphrase back every few minutes ("So if I understand…"). Capture their **exact words**. Note what you *didn't* get.

| ✅ Commit to — only this | ❌ Avoid |
|---|---|
| "Thank you, that's really helpful. I'll send a recap by [EOD / tomorrow] and come back with any questions." | Scope, dates, effort, feasibility, opinions on the solution, or a "yes" to anything |

**Safe phrases:**
- "Let me take that away and come back to you."
- "I want to be sure we do this properly, so let me check with the team."
- "I don't know yet. Let me find out."

### After

| When | Activity | Who |
|---|---|---|
| Within an hour | Write up notes while fresh. Note which TRACES letters you got, and which you didn't. **Most will be unanswered, and that's the point.** | **[Solo]** |
| Same day | Send the recap with specific follow-up questions. | **[Stakeholder]** |
| Same day | Tell your PM or lead what was asked, and what was (and wasn't) committed. | **[Team]** |
| Within 1–2 days | Do the prep you couldn't do: secondary research + past-work review (Fresh start prep), or check for existing synthesis (Informed prep). | **[Solo]** |
| Next | Schedule a **proper follow-up call**. | **[Team] + [Stakeholder]** |

> **The ad hoc call turns into a Fresh start or an Informed call. That follow-up is where requirement definition really happens.**

## 10. The requirement one-pager

Every requirement call ends in one artifact. It is not throwaway — it is the starting point for interpretation and design.

**Contents:**

1. **The problem as the client sees it** — in their words, not yours
2. **Users** — who does the work, who's affected, who decides
3. **Constraints** — edges, what's been tried, the existing product / design system
4. **Success** — the measurable result, for user and business
5. **Open questions** — *required*. The letters you could not answer, or could only answer partly.
6. *(Informed by synthesis only)* **Which insight supports each requirement**

Plus, alongside it: the **assumption log** — every claim you tagged as assumption or opinion rather than fact.

> **Open questions are required. An empty letter is a valid output.**

## 10b. Before you start designing: the completion check

Don't open a design file until you can honestly tick these. Where you can't, **name the assumption in writing** and carry on — the sin isn't designing with gaps, it's designing with gaps nobody has named.

### The minimum bar

- [ ] **T** — I can name the event that triggered the request, *and* describe the workflow failure underneath it.
- [ ] **R** — I have one user-side change and one business-side change, each with a signal we could actually measure.
- [ ] **A** — I can name the primary role, their environment and device, the downstream roles, and the decision maker.
- [ ] **C** — Every claim in the one-pager is tagged fact / assumption / opinion, and the three riskiest assumptions are listed with "what breaks if wrong".
- [ ] **E** — I know the hard constraints, what must not change, the main edge cases, and what's been tried before.
- [ ] **S** — I know who approves, the review rhythm, and the next checkpoint.
- [ ] **+1 / +2 / +3** — constraints, what already exists in the product and design system, and what's been rejected.
- [ ] I have a **problem statement** naming user, obstacle, impact and measure — with no solution in it.
- [ ] Every requirement traces to **evidence or a named assumption**. Nothing is unattributed.
- [ ] For every piece of research I'm leaning on, I know whether it's **data, a finding, or an insight** — and only insights are carrying design directions.

### If you can't tick them

1. Name the gap — which letter, which side.
2. Write the assumption you'd be designing on.
3. Decide with the team: design on the stated assumption, or research first?
4. Put the open question in the recap, and the assumption in your annotations.
5. Revisit it at the next checkpoint. An assumption that survives three reviews unchallenged has quietly become a "fact" — go back and check it.

> **Three fast diagnostics:** no **R** and you can't defend any design later. No **C** and you're about to treat an opinion as a fact. No **S** and the work can be right and still get rejected.

## 11. Data vs findings vs insights

In Part 1 you tagged claims as fact, assumption or opinion. Research outputs work the same way — **in layers**. Each layer does a different job, and mixing them up is how designs get dismissed.

> **When you brief a teammate — or an AI tool — say which layer you're handing over.**

### The three layers

| Layer | What it is | Answers | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **01 · Data** | Raw, unanalysed observations: quotes, clicks, notes, metrics | *What was recorded?* | Session 4: "I never know if this amount is normal for her." · Session 2: manager opens the policy PDF in a second tab. · Analytics: 38% of claims are returned for missing information. |
| **02 · Finding** | A pattern across comparable data points. Says *what* happened, not *why*. | *What happened?* | 6 of 9 managers left the approval screen to check policy or ask finance. 2 approved without checking. |
| **03 · Insight** | An explanation **plus** an opportunity, using other research and business context | *Why, and so what?* | See below |

### The example insight, unpacked

*(Running example: a company's expense-approval tool for managers.)*

| | |
|---|---|
| **What** | Managers stall, or approve blind. |
| **Why** | The approval screen shows an amount and a receipt, but no policy or norm context. |
| **So what** | Reimbursement slows. Compliance risk lands on finance. |
| **Opportunity** | Bring decision context to the point of approval. |

**The insight formula:** **what happened + why + so what**
- *What happened* is the pattern (the finding).
- *Why* is beliefs, expectations, or triggers.
- *So what* is impact on the user **and** the business.

Note what it took to get there: the finding alone couldn't support a decision because it had no context. It needed finance's goals — faster reimbursement, fewer audit exceptions — to become an opportunity. (This is NN/g's point about findings and context.)

### The line that matters

> **Data and findings are knowledge. They tell us what is true.**
> **Insights tell us what to do about it.**

And: **insights narrow the design space. They don't pick the answer. Testing does.**

### Quick tests for classifying anything you hear

| If it… | It's probably a… |
|---|---|
| Records one thing that happened | **Data** |
| Summarises a pattern but has no "because" | **Finding** |
| Has "because" **and** "which means", tied to a user need and a business goal | **Insight** |
| Rests on one person's comment | An observation — flag it as a **hypothesis**, not a pattern |

### Why this matters more now, not less

AI is in the design workflow — summaries, flows and rationale in seconds. But:

1. **It works fast.**
2. **It uses whatever we give it.** A single quote can be presented with the confidence of a pattern.
3. **So we choose the input** — which research, at which layer, for which decision.

> **AI can summarise what's true. Deciding what to do stays with us.**
> Speed doesn't replace judgment. It makes judgment matter more.

### Bias guardrail

Insights always carry some researcher bias (NN/g). Reduce it with **triangulation**: multiple sources, multiple methods, or multiple analysts. The same applies to AI-assisted synthesis — give it explicit instructions and guardrails, and check its output against the raw layer so that misleading or hallucinated "insights" don't enter the base.

## 12. Defending each layer under challenge

This is the payoff. Different challenges attack different layers, and each has its own response.

| Stakeholder challenge | Layer attacked | How to respond |
|---|---|---|
| **"Where did that come from?"** | Data | Trace it to the session, quote or metric. |
| **"Is that statistically significant?"** | Finding | Qualitative work aims for a **consistent pattern (saturation)**, not significance. Rule of thumb: ~5 users per segment surface major issues; 10–15 usually reach saturation. Add **triangulation** ("the analytics agree with the sessions"), then reframe in consequences: *"If six of nine managers hit the same wall, what does that cost us in delayed reimbursements?"* |
| **"Why this direction?"** | Insight | Walk the **why** and the **so-what**, and name the **business goal** it serves. |
| **"That's just one person's opinion."** | Data vs finding | Say how many people it was. If it was one, say so — and label it a **hypothesis**. |
| **"Why did you design it this way?"** | The whole chain | *"Here's what we heard, here's what we know, and here's how it traces."* |

## 13. How designers actually use an insight

> An insight isn't a slide. It's a working tool. There are five ways you use one.

| Use | What you do |
|---|---|
| **Shape requirements** | Compare it to the TRACES one-pager. Does it **confirm, challenge or extend** what the client said? |
| **Frame the problem** | Feed it into the insight-to-direction chain (below). |
| **Test ideas** | Ask of any idea: *"Does this address the why?"* |
| **Justify decisions** | Cite it in annotations, tickets and reviews. |
| **Set success criteria** | The **"so what"** becomes the metric. |

### A usable insight has

- A specific **user and situation**
- A **why**
- A **so-what** tied to a goal
- **Traceable evidence**
- A stated **confidence level**
- A **direction**, not a solution

**Personas as a filter:** use them to test ideas and directions ("would Priya do this?"), not as a slide shown once.

## 14. The insight-to-direction path

**The rule:** *you can only write the next step if the previous one supports it. If you can't write it, that isn't failure — it tells you what you're missing.*

| Step | Job in the chain | Format | Passes when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1 · Insight** | Explains why, and why it matters | What happened + why + so what | Traceable to data |
| **2 · JTBD** | What the user is trying to get done — no solution | "When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]" *(optional: "without [struggle]")* | A different solution could satisfy it, and it's one job at the right altitude |
| **3 · Problem statement** | What's in the way, who for, why it matters | "[User] needs to [job], but [obstacle — from the insight's *why*], which leads to [impact — from the *so-what*]. Solved when [measure]." | Names user, obstacle, impact and measure — and contains no solution |
| **4 · HMW** | Opens the problem up for ideation | "How might we [outcome-focused action] for [user] so that [outcome]?" | Passes the HMW checklist below |
| **5 · UX opportunity** | Directions worth exploring | Short direction statements | A direction, not a finished screen |
| **6 · AI opportunity** | Tests whether AI is the best way to pursue a direction | PAIR-style questions (below) | AI adds value over a simpler approach |

*(The problem-statement formula is our adaptation of IxDF's user + need + insight — say so, rather than letting people hunt for it in a textbook.)*

**Why this order:** each step adds exactly one thing. The insight gives the *why*; the JTBD gives the *goal*; the problem statement gives the *obstacle, impact and measure*; the HMW converts it into an open question. Opportunities come after HMW because they're the output of ideation. The AI check comes last because it's judged against concrete directions.

**Shortcut:** if time is tight, NN/g goes insight → HMW directly. You lose the explicit goal and success measure — so use it only when those are already clear.

### Turning each step into the next

#### Insight → JTBD

1. Pull out the **user and situation** (the trigger).
2. Ask what they are trying to **get done**.
3. Strip out any feature, and ask "why?" until you reach the **goal**.
4. Add the **outcome**, and optionally the **struggle**.

> *"When I'm reviewing a team member's expense claim, I want to know whether it's within policy and normal for them, so I can approve or return it confidently without chasing finance."*

The insight told us why they struggle. The job tells us what they're trying to do. Notice it mentions **no screen, no button, no AI**. "I want a policy pop-up" would be a solution, not a job.

#### JTBD → Problem statement

> *"Managers who approve team expenses need to judge quickly whether a claim is acceptable, but the approval screen shows only an amount and a receipt. They leave to check policy, ask finance, or approve blind. This delays reimbursement (median 2.3 days) and risks non-compliant spend. We'll know it's solved when approval time and send-backs fall without more policy exceptions."*

The **need** comes from the job, the **obstacle** from the insight's why, the **impact** from the so-what. And from Part 1: the **Result** and **Edges** on the requirement one-pager supply the success measure and the constraints.

#### Problem statement → HMW

Write 3–5, then pick 1–2. **NN/g's checklist:** based on a real problem or insight · tracks the desired outcome · phrased positively · broad enough for many ideas · suggests no solution.

| HMW | Verdict |
|---|---|
| How might we add a policy tooltip to the approval screen? | ❌ Solution baked in |
| How might we stop managers approving blind? | ❌ Negative and symptom-focused |
| How might we improve approvals? | ❌ Too broad |
| How might we help managers feel confident a claim is fine to approve? | ✅ Outcome-focused and open |
| How might we make a claim's fit with policy clear at the moment of decision? | ✅ Specific but still open |

> **The problem statement pins down. The HMW opens up.**

#### HMW → UX opportunity

Directions, not designs:
- Policy check shown inline
- Outlier flags against team norms
- One-tap return with reason templates
- A completeness check before submission *(this one addresses the 38% returns)*

Choose between directions using the evidence, the constraints from Part 1, and the persona filter.

> **An opportunity is a direction to test, not a decision. The insight narrows the options. Testing picks.**

#### UX opportunity → AI opportunity

Say first: **"No AI here" is a valid answer.**

Then, based on Google PAIR:

1. **Is there real evidence of the user need?** *(Yes — from the insight.)*
2. **Does AI add unique value, or would a simpler approach do?** *(Rules can check clear policy cases. AI may add value for messy receipts and free-text policy.)*
3. **Automate or augment?** *(Augment: the manager still decides.)*
4. **What does a wrong answer cost?** *(A false "looks fine" recreates blind approval.)*
5. **Is there a graceful fallback to a non-AI path?**

> We check AI last, against concrete directions, so it has to earn its place. The aim is to spot AI **inside the workflow** from the start — not bolt it on at the end.

## 15. Keeping insights visible and current

**The rule:** every design decision should trace back to an insight.

> If you can't trace a decision, either the insight base has a gap, or the decision is an assumption. **Both are worth knowing.**

**How it stays alive:**

- Insights live in the **shared project context**, not in someone's local file.
- They get referenced in **prototype annotations**.
- They show up in **tickets**.
- After every call, they get re-tagged: **confirmed / challenged / new / outdated**.
- When AI is used to read or summarise the base, it needs explicit instructions and guardrails so it doesn't surface biased, misleading or hallucinated insights.

## 16. Using TRACES outside requirement calls

TRACES is written for calls, but it is really a **completeness check on what you know about a problem**. Anywhere a request arrives or a decision gets made, you can run the six letters and see where the holes are — no stakeholder required.

### How to run it solo

Take any request, ticket, brief or half-formed idea. Write the six letters down the page. Fill in what you can **from what already exists**. Whatever stays empty is your research agenda, your question list, or your stated assumptions — your choice, but you now have to make it deliberately.

The two-sided rule still applies: fill each letter once for **the request** (the thing being asked for) and once for **the workflow** (the process underneath).

### Three diagnostics worth memorising

1. **No R** → you cannot define success, so you cannot defend any design later. Fix this first.
2. **No C** → you are about to treat an opinion as a fact. The fastest fix is to label it, out loud, as an assumption.
3. **No S** → the work may be correct and still get rejected, because the wrong person saw it at the wrong time.

### Pairing TRACES with the three layers

When you fill TRACES from existing material rather than from a call, label **what layer each entry came from**:

- A letter filled from **data** → one quote or one metric. Treat as a hypothesis.
- A letter filled from a **finding** → a pattern. Solid on *what*, silent on *why*.
- A letter filled from an **insight** → you have why and so-what. This is the only kind of entry that can carry a design direction.
- A letter filled from a **stakeholder's belief** → that's an opinion. Label it as one and move on.

This combination — six letters, each with a source and a layer label — is the shortest honest summary of "what we know about this problem" that exists. It's what you want on the wall before anyone opens Figma.
